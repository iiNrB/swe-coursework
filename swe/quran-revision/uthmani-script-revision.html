This is how Bangladesh actually teaches it

You remembered correctly. Qaida (letter recognition and joining) followed by Ampara (Juz Amma, practiced in short surahs) is the standard route across Bangladesh, India, Pakistan and Indonesia. The Mushaf used at that stage is printed in Indo-Pak script — the regional style, not the Uthmani/Madani style used in the Gulf and increasingly worldwide. The alphabet, the joining rules, the makhraj (articulation points) and the tajweed rules you learned are identical in both — nothing there needs relearning. What differs is a handful of conventions in how the text is marked and printed. That's the actual gap to close, and it's much smaller than starting over.

Qaida quick revision — the 28 letters

Each card shows how the letter looks alone, then joined at the start, middle and end of a word (the dash marks the connection point). Letters marked NC don't connect to the letter after them, even mid-word — you already know this from Qaida, this is just to jog it back.

Harakat and marks revision

The vowel signs and marks that turn letters into recitation — same in every script.

بَ

Fatha — short "a"

A small diagonal stroke above the letter.

بِ

Kasra — short "i"

The same stroke, below the letter.

بُ

Damma — short "u"

A small loop above the letter.

بْ

Sukun — no vowel

A small circle; the letter is pronounced with no vowel sound.

بّ

Shaddah — doubling

The letter's sound is held and doubled.

بً بٍ بٌ

Tanween — an / in / un

Doubled fatha/kasra/damma, read as a nasal "n" ending.

با بو بي

Madd letters — long vowels

Alif, waw and ya after a matching short vowel stretch it: aa, uu, ii.

هٰذَا

Dagger alif — a hidden "aa"

A small vertical stroke stands in for a full alif. You'll see this often in both scripts — e.g. in هَٰذَا (haadhaa) and ٱلرَّحْمَٰن (ar-Rahmaan).

Indo-Pak vs. Uthmani — what actually changes

Side by side: the habits your Ampara built, and what the Uthmani/Madani Mushaf does differently. None of this changes how the words sound — only how they're marked and laid out on the page.

What you learned

Indo-Pak script

  • Used across Bangladesh, India, Pakistan, Indonesia — this is almost certainly what your Ampara and early Mushaf were printed in.
  • Hamzat-ul-wasl (the "connecting" alif at the start of words like ٱبْنُ) is usually left as a plain alif. The rule for when to pronounce it is taught separately rather than marked on the page.
  • Page and line layout varies by publisher — not standardized to a fixed page count.
  • Letterforms follow a regional calligraphic style, slightly bolder and more condensed than Gulf printings.
What's new

Uthmani / Madani script

  • The King Fahd Complex (Madinah) standard — the script most widely used internationally and in most Tajweed courses and apps today.
  • Hamzat-ul-wasl is marked directly on the alif with a small head (ٱ), so the page itself tells you whether to pronounce it, instead of relying on a memorized rule.
  • Standardized to exactly 604 pages, 15 lines per page, with each Juz starting on a new page — useful for memorization and for following along with reciters.
  • A fixed set of stop signs (waqf marks) is used consistently: مـ stop, لا don't stop, ج optional stop, صلى / قلى preferable to stop/continue, ۛ ۛ stop at one of a pair.
These are the well-documented, structural differences. Exact local conventions can vary slightly between printings and teachers — confirm the fine details with your Hifz/Tajweed teacher or a qualified aalim rather than relying on this page alone for anything you'll recite from memory.

How to actually revise this

1

Open a Uthmani-script Mushaf — the Quran.com app/site defaults to it, or search for "Madani Mushaf" — and turn to a short surah from Juz Amma you already know well from Ampara.

2

Read it aloud from memory first, the way you learned it. Then read it again looking carefully at the page, slowing down anywhere the shape looks unfamiliar.

3

Whenever something looks different, check it against the comparison table above — it's almost always a hamzat-ul-wasl mark or a dagger alif, not a new sound.

4

Once a handful of short surahs feel natural in Uthmani script, move to longer, less-memorized passages — the differences stop being noticeable within a few pages.
